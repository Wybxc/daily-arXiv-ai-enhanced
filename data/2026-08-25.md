Motivation: 证明辅助工具在课堂数学教育中具有巨大潜力（即时反馈和渐进式证明脚手架），但此前难以在课堂中部署，主要原因有二：(1) 学生难以掌握完整功能证明辅助工具的复杂性；(2) 证明辅助工具被认为在学习支持和向无工具的纸笔测试知识迁移方面效果不佳。作者希望设计一个专门为课堂教学优化的证明辅助工具。

Method: 作者基于此前在课堂中部署证明辅助工具的观察，综合出设计标准（工具的易用性、学生对数学思想的参与度、向纸笔证明的迁移、课堂后勤）。采用迭代设计评估流程，先后在两个不同的班级部署Hazel Prover，并对细粒度使用日志、调查数据和学生考试答案进行深入分析。

Result: 分析显示学生能有效学习使用该工具，并在解决归纳证明问题过程中能力显著提升。然而，第一个设计未能有效实现向纸笔证明的迁移。作者假设原因是工具在等式推理步骤中提供了过多帮助。基于这一负结果，作者在第二次部署中强制学生更手动地参与等式推理步骤，结果实现了更有效的知识迁移。

Conclusion: 研究结果表明，证明辅助工具在课堂中的有效性不仅取决于工具的可用性，还取决于工具自动化程度与学生学习参与度之间的平衡。过度的自动化可能损害知识向纸笔证明的迁移能力。作者认为其分析为未来面向多种数学领域的课堂证明辅助工具设计者提供了可推广的洞见。

Abstract: Proof assistants offer instant feedback and incremental proof scaffolding to users. Both of these features have long held promise in improving mathematics education in classroom settings, where manual grading is costly, and students often struggle with knowing how to proceed in their proof. However, they have been difficult to deploy in classroom settings due to two main concerns: (i) students struggle with the intricacies of full-scale proof assistants; and (ii) proof assistants are ineffective in support of student learning, and knowledge transfer to on-paper assessments without the tool.
  We present Hazel Prover, a classroom proof assistant for teaching equational and inductive reasoning, with a design informed by criteria encompassing ease-of-use of the tool, student engagement with underlying mathematical ideas, transfer to pen-and-paper proof, and classroom logistics. We synthesized these criteria from observations made in prior deployments of proof assistants to the classroom. We engaged in an iterative design and evaluation process, deploying Hazel Prover in two different classes and conducting in-depth analyses of fine-grained usage logs, survey data, and student exam responses. Our analysis demonstrates that students were able to learn to use the tool effectively, and that students became more capable with inductive proof as they progressed through problems. However, the first design did not effectively achieve transfer to pen-and-paper proofs. We hypothesized that this was due to the tool offering too much help to students in the equational reasoning steps. Based on this negative result, we enforced more manual student engagement with equational steps, which led to more effective transfer in the second deployment. We believe that our analyses offer generalizable insights relevant to the designers of future classroom proof assistants for a variety of mathematical domains.
